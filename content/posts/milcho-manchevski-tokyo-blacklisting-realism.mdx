Milcho Manchevski, the Macedonian‑American auteur behind the Oscar‑nominated “Before the Rain,” premiered his eighth feature, “Sister Brother Manhole Cover,” in competition at the Tokyo International Film Festival on Oct. 30. In a candid interview with The Hollywood Reporter, the 67‑year‑old director opened up about the film’s dark‑comedy take on fraternal love, the widening wealth gap, and the persistent specter of industry blacklisting. He also unpacked his uneasy relationship with neo‑social realism and why he ventured into documentary filmmaking despite his skepticism. The conversation arrives at a moment when message‑driven cinema is proliferating, making his perspective especially relevant for filmmakers and audiences alike.

<Callout type="takeaway">Manchevski uses satire to expose social inequities while defending artistic independence in a climate of industry blacklisting.</Callout>

## What happened
Manchevski split his time between Macedonia and New York before returning to Tokyo with “Sister Brother Manhole Cover,” a dark comedy that meditates on sister‑brother love, motherhood, and the growing wealth gap. The film, his eighth feature, debuted on Oct. 30 and entered the competition lineup, marking his second appearance at the festival after the 2022 competition run of “Kaymak.”

During the interview, he explained that the story grew from Macedonian folk songs about sisterly devotion, which he found both profound and unsettling. He described the film as a satire‑driven character study that avoids preaching, aiming instead for beauty amid social critique. Manchevski also addressed personal experiences of being blacklisted, the recurring presence of mother figures in his work, and his cautious embrace of neo‑social realism despite lingering doubts about documentary form.

## Why it matters
Manchevski’s willingness to discuss blacklisting shines a light on informal industry gatekeeping that can silence dissenting voices, a concern that resonates beyond the Balkan cinema community. His blend of satire with social commentary challenges the prevailing trend of overtly didactic films, suggesting a path where humor and empathy coexist with critique. By confronting the wealth gap through a personal narrative, the film invites global audiences to reflect on systemic inequality without sacrificing artistic nuance.
