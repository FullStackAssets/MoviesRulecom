The buzz around Indian cinema’s newest action franchise has taken a surprising turn toward wildlife advocacy. At Deadline’s Local to Global event in Bruges, actor‑director Anshuman Jha and actress Sarah‑Jane Dias sat down to discuss their sequel Lakadbaggha 2: The Monkey Business. The film, fresh from its runner‑up Audience Choice slot at the Razor Reel Flanders Film Festival, pits a martial‑arts‑trained hero against an illegal animal‑trade ring in Indonesia. Beyond the high‑octane fights, the duo emphasizes the real‑world plight of the Yaki monkey, whose numbers have plummeted from 50,000 in 1990 to just 4,500 today. Their message signals a shift where blockbuster entertainment can also champion conservation.

<Callout type="takeaway">Lakadbaggha 2 shows that big‑budget action can carry a powerful animal‑rights message.</Callout>

## What happened
During the interview, Jha explained that the franchise’s hero Arjun Bakshi, first introduced as a vegan vigilante in Kolkata, now travels to North Sulawesi after his fiancée disappears. The sequel’s plot centers on an underground fight tournament that doubles as an auction for the last surviving Yaki monkey, intertwining personal stakes with a broader ecological crisis.

Jha also highlighted the film’s commitment to authentic Indian martial arts, drawing on Kalaripayattu—a centuries‑old discipline rarely seen on screen. Produced by First Ray Films and Matta Cinema, the movie aims to blend 1980s pulp action aesthetics with a modern conservation narrative.

The project’s world premiere at the Razor Reel Flanders Film Festival earned a runner‑up Audience Choice Award, indicating strong audience resonance with its hybrid genre.

## Why it matters
Lakadbaggha 2 is more than a sequel; it positions Indian martial‑arts heritage within a global action framework while foregrounding a pressing environmental issue. By spotlighting the Yaki monkey’s dramatic decline, the film leverages mainstream appeal to educate viewers about illegal wildlife trade. Its success could encourage other producers to embed authentic cultural practices and conservation themes into genre cinema, expanding both representation and advocacy.
