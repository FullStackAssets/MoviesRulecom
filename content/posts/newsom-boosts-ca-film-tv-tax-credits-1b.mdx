Just days before California’s gubernatorial election, Gov. Gavin Newsom announced a surprise plan to lift the state’s film and TV tax credit program to more than $1 billion. The proposal would raise the current $750 million allocation, which was only doubled last year from $330 million, to a level that would make California’s incentive the largest capped program in the nation. If enacted, the boost aims to attract new productions, restore jobs lost in recent years, and position the state as a dominant West Coast hub for streaming and studio projects.

<Callout type="takeaway">California’s film‑TV tax credit could jump past $1 billion, reshaping the state’s production landscape.</Callout>

## What happened
Governor Newsom unveiled the plan in a Monday morning briefing, stating that “at least $1 billion in tax credits means more investment flowing into our economy and more opportunity across the industry.” The proposal would increase the capped budget from $750 million to over $1 billion, building on the 2025 expansion that lifted the program from $330 million.

The announcement comes amid a high‑stakes midterm election and a broader bipartisan push for a federal film and TV tax credit, backed by a coalition that includes both Democratic and Republican leaders. Industry analysts note that California has lost more than 50,000 production‑related jobs over the past six years, a decline the state hopes to reverse with the new funding.

## Why it matters
A larger credit pool could make California more competitive against uncapped programs in Georgia and the expanding incentives in New York, potentially drawing back projects that have migrated west. The infusion of tax credits is also expected to generate ancillary economic activity, from local hiring to increased spending on services and infrastructure. However, the expansion depends on state budget approval and the passage of complementary federal legislation, creating uncertainty for producers planning future shoots.

## How to think about it
Production companies should model budgets with both the current $750 million cap and the proposed $1 billion ceiling to assess eligibility and timing. Policymakers need to weigh short‑term fiscal costs against long‑term economic gains from a revitalized industry. Investors can monitor the legislative timeline to gauge risk before committing capital to California‑based projects.

<Callout type="warning">The increase will not take effect until the state legislature approves the budget and the federal credit bill passes, so firms should not assume funding is guaranteed.</Callout>
