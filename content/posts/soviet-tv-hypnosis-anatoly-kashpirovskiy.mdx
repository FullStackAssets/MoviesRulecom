During the late 1980s Soviet state television turned a little‑known medical doctor into a nightly sensation. Anatoly Kashpirovskiy, a self‑styled mass‑hypnosis artist, captivated millions with his trance‑inducing performances broadcast across the bloc. His shows coincided with the political upheaval that would soon dissolve the USSR, making his popularity a cultural barometer of a society in flux. For today’s streaming‑savvy audience, the story offers a rare glimpse into how a single televised figure can shape collective memory.
<Callout type="takeaway">Soviet TV’s hypnotic star shows how mass media can forge a shared cultural moment.</Callout>
## What happened
Kashpirovskiy trained as a physician and began experimenting with hypnosis to keep medical students awake during lectures. When state TV channels invited him to demonstrate his techniques, his televised sessions drew unprecedented viewership, turning his name into a household word across the Soviet Union.
Polish director Lukasz Konopa recalls watching the broadcasts as a seven‑year‑old in a gray village, noting that the simultaneous demand on the power grid caused lights to flicker in his apartment. Decades later, the director rediscovered Kashpirovskiy during the 2020 lockdown, prompting him to program the documentary “Kashpirovskiy. Under the Spell” for the Ji.hlava Testimonies section, positioning the hypnotist as a symbol of the transition from communism to early capitalism.
## Why it matters
The phenomenon illustrates the power of a single televised event to create a pan‑Soviet cultural touchstone, prefiguring today’s binge‑watch moments on streaming platforms. It also reveals how media can both reflect and accelerate social change, as audiences collectively experienced a spectacle that coincided with the crumbling of a political system. Understanding this history helps viewers recognize similar dynamics in contemporary digital media.
