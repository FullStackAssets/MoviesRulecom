The modern fairy‑tale film Mo Amor, directed by Estonia’s Eeva Mägi, opened the Critics’ Picks competition at the 30th Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival on Nov. 16, 2026. The story follows Princess Niina, who discards her crown to confront three former suitors in a summer house, prompting a surreal reunion of memory, myth and love. By foregrounding transgender experiences and fluid identities, the film pushes Estonian cinema into a broader conversation about love’s many forms. Its premiere follows Mägi’s previous success with Mo Papa, which earned a special mention in the festival’s Critics’ Picks last year. The buzz around Mo Amor signals a growing appetite for inclusive narratives on the international festival circuit.
<Callout type="takeaway">Mo Amor uses a fairy‑tale framework to examine how love survives when we reveal our true selves.</Callout>
## What happened
Mo Amor had its world premiere on Monday, Nov. 16, 2026, as part of the Critics’ Picks competition at the 30th edition of the Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival (PÖFF). Writer‑director Eeva Mägi co‑produced the film with cinematographer Sten‑Johan Lill, and editor Jette Keedus completed the post‑production. The cast includes Ester Kuntu, Jarmo Reha, Tambet Tuisk, Andres Mähar, Sander Roosimägi and Ivor Mikker, many of whom appeared in Mägi’s earlier work Mo Papa.
The narrative is introduced as a modern fairy‑tale: Princess Niina is courted by three suitors—the Mentor, the Writer and the Stonemason—yet she remains restless. She ultimately sheds her crown, invites the suitors to a secluded summer home, and the reunion triggers an intertwining of memory, myth and reality, allowing old wounds to surface and love to take unexpected forms.
In an interview with The Hollywood Reporter, Mägi explained that the film stems from her desire to blend personal stories with societal questions, asking what happens when love endures the truth of who we really are.
## Why it matters
Mo Amor’s focus on transgender and fluid identities marks a significant step for Estonian cinema, which has historically produced more conventional narratives. By placing these themes inside a fairy‑tale structure, the film makes complex social issues accessible to a broader audience while preserving artistic nuance. The festival platform amplifies the conversation, offering international distributors a clear signal that inclusive storytelling can resonate on the global stage. Moreover, the film’s success could encourage other Baltic creators to explore marginalized perspectives, potentially reshaping regional cinematic trends.
