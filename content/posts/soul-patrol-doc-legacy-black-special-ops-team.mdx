The new documentary Soul Patrol has hit theaters, marking the first wide‑release look at Team 2/6, the U.S. Army’s inaugural all‑Black special‑operations unit in Vietnam. Directed by Sundance award‑winner J.M. Harper, the film opened on November 6 in the United States and will roll out internationally starting November 13 in London. By pairing never‑before‑seen archival footage with contemporary interviews and inventive reenactments, the film captures surviving veterans as they reunite and recount their wartime experience. Its timing on the war’s 50th anniversary forces a fresh reckoning with the contradictions of fighting for freedom abroad while confronting segregation at home. The story, rooted in Ed Emanuel’s 2003 memoir, offers a rare glimpse into a chapter of military and civil‑rights history that has long been overlooked.

<Callout type="takeaway">Soul Patrol reveals how a pioneering Black unit fought abroad while confronting racism at home.</Callout>

## What happened
Soul Patrol premiered in U.S. theaters on November 6 through Kino Lorber, followed by an international debut at DocHouse London on November 13. The documentary weaves together archival photographs, moving images, and newly filmed interviews with the surviving members of Team 2/6, the first all‑Black Long‑Range Reconnaissance Patrol (LRRP) unit deployed in Vietnam.

Harper spent six years building trust with veteran Ed Emanuel, the author of the memoir that sparked the project, and conducted weekly calls to shape the narrative. The film’s structure follows the veterans as they gather in a hotel conference room, sharing personal recollections that oscillate between pride, sorrow, and disbelief, while the director juxtaposes these testimonies with reenactments that visualize the unit’s covert missions.

## Why it matters
Soul Patrol forces viewers to confront the paradox of Black soldiers defending a nation that denied them basic civil rights, highlighting how their service both challenged and reinforced broader struggles for racial equality. By bringing this hidden history to a mainstream audience, the documentary expands the public’s understanding of the Vietnam War’s social dimensions and underscores the ongoing relevance of representation in military narratives.

## How to think about it
Approach the film as both a historical case study and a catalyst for broader conversations about representation.
