When Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim released ‘You Can See Everything,’ it pulled in more than $15 million on opening weekend, eclipsing every documentary debut since 2004. The three‑hour film opened on fewer than 900 screens, a limited rollout that still outperformed larger releases like the $7.1 million opening of Amazon MGM’s ‘Melania.’ Its 99 % Rotten Tomatoes score and 4.8 rating on Letterboxd sparked a wave of sold‑out screenings in New York, Los Angeles and other major markets. The unprecedented box‑office performance signals a shift in how audiences and studios view documentary potential.
<Callout type="takeaway">‘You Can See Everything’ proves a limited‑release documentary can dominate the box office.</Callout>
## What happened
The documentary opened to $15 million in ticket sales, the biggest opening for a documentary in more than 20 years and the largest limited‑release debut on fewer than 1,000 screens since “Borat” in 2006. It debuted on under 900 screens after a surprise premiere at the Telluride Film Festival, and weekend screenings in New York and Los Angeles sold out despite the three‑hour runtime.
Filmmakers Fielder and Oppenheim spent years filming Elizabeth Holmes and her partner Billy Evans in secret, capturing interviews just weeks before Holmes began her 2023 prison term. Critics responded enthusiastically, noting the film’s genre‑bending approach and awarding it a 99 % fresh rating on Rotten Tomatoes and a 4.8 score on Letterboxd.
In comparison, Amazon MGM’s “Melania” opened on twice as many screens but earned only $7.1 million, underscoring the unique pull of “You Can See Everything.” The film finished second at the North American weekend box office behind Paramount’s “Street Fighter,” which earned $30 million on 3,575 screens.
## Why it matters
The success challenges the conventional wisdom that documentaries need wide releases to achieve blockbuster numbers, giving studios like A24 confidence to invest in ambitious, limited‑run projects. High critical scores combined with strong audience demand suggest a growing appetite for documentary storytelling that blurs the line between investigative journalism and narrative cinema. The performance also fuels speculation about awards potential, as box‑office momentum often translates into greater visibility during Oscar season.

## How to think about it
Filmmakers should consider pairing a festival debut with a tightly controlled limited release to generate urgency and media coverage. Strong pre‑release marketing that highlights unique access—such as exclusive interviews with high‑profile subjects—can amplify word‑of‑mouth and fill seats despite a modest screen count. Distributors might also leverage high critical scores in advertising to attract audiences who normally skip documentaries, positioning the film as a must‑see cultural event rather than a niche offering.
