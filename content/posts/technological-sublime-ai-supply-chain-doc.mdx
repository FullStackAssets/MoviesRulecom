The upcoming documentary *The Technological Sublime* is set to premiere at Taiwan’s Creative Content Fest, pulling back the curtain on the tangible resources that power artificial intelligence. Director Jannes Callens tracks the journey from quartz mines in the Appalachian region, through Taiwan’s chip fabs, Chile’s lithium fields, undersea fiber cables, and finally to Kenyan click‑workers who label data. By visualizing this hidden network, the film challenges the common perception of AI as an ethereal, “green” technology. Its debut arrives as the AI debate intensifies around sustainability and ethical labor practices. Audiences will see how each link in the chain carries environmental and human costs that are rarely discussed.

<Callout type="takeaway">AI’s environmental and labor impact is rooted in a physical supply chain that most users never see.</Callout>

## What happened
*The Technological Sublime* follows a concrete trail: quartz is extracted from Appalachian mines, refined and shipped to Taiwan where semiconductor factories carve the chips that power modern AI models. From there, the documentary moves to Chile’s lithium extraction sites, the raw material for batteries that store AI‑driven devices. Undersea cables then carry massive data streams across oceans, while Kenyan click‑workers perform the painstaking task of labeling and curating the data that trains machine‑learning algorithms. Director Callens says he was drawn to the subject because “people tend to talk about AI as if it were something very abstract and invisible.”

The film’s first scenes have already been shot, and the production team is seeking final financing to complete the project. Callens, whose previous documentary *Dor (Longing)* earned a Best Belgian Film award, hopes to blend artistic storytelling with investigative depth, offering viewers a nuanced view of AI’s hidden costs.

## Why it matters
Understanding the material and labor inputs behind AI reframes the conversation from abstract ethics to concrete accountability. The mining of quartz and lithium contributes to habitat disruption and carbon emissions, while the reliance on low‑paid click‑workers raises questions about fair labor standards in the data economy. For policymakers, investors, and everyday users, recognizing these links can inform more responsible procurement, regulation, and personal consumption choices. Moreover, the documentary arrives at a moment when tech companies are promoting “green AI” initiatives; exposing the full supply chain provides a reality check against marketing narratives.

## How to think about it
When you watch *The Technological Sublime*, map each scene to a broader question: Who extracts the raw material? What energy is used in processing? Who is paid for the data work, and under what conditions? Use that mental checklist to evaluate AI products you use—look for transparency reports, sustainability certifications, and fair‑labor statements. If a company’s AI claims lack supply‑chain disclosure, consider alternatives or push for greater accountability.

<Callout type="warning">The documentary reflects the director’s perspective and may not represent every stakeholder in the AI supply chain; treat its conclusions as a starting point for further research.</Callout>
