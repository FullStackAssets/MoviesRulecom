At this year’s International Documentary Film Festival (IDFA), a heated panel confronted the growing practice of documentary creators licensing their archival footage to AI aggregators. Panelists, including rights‑management expert Wendy Bernfield, explained that AI systems have been scraping film material for over three years, often without clear consent. The discussion highlighted recent examples such as Ava DuVernay’s use of AI‑generated animation in Netflix’s documentary “14th,” and the 2024 backlash against Piotr Winiewicz’s AI‑driven experiment “About a Hero.” Understanding how these deals affect revenue, creative control, and the ethical landscape is now essential for anyone working in nonfiction cinema.

<Callout type="takeaway">Selling footage to AI aggregators can unlock new income streams but also jeopardizes rights and artistic integrity.</Callout>

## What happened
During the IDFA session titled “Is AI Stealing My Documentary?” Bernfield warned that AI has been “scraping or stealing before we were even aware enough about it,” likening the early stage to the unregulated days of YouTube.

Since then, AI aggregators have emerged as platforms that bundle outputs from multiple models, offering filmmakers a single point of contact for licensing their clips.

Some creators, like DuVernay, have begun to sell limited rights to animate public‑domain photographs, while others remain wary after the controversy surrounding Winiewicz’s experiment, which prompted prominent directors such as Werner Herzog to publicly reject AI‑generated cinema.

## Why it matters
The shift reshapes how documentary archives generate revenue, moving from one‑off sales to recurring licensing fees tied to AI model training. It also raises questions about who controls the narrative context when footage is repurposed by machines, potentially eroding the trust that audiences place in nonfiction storytelling. Moreover, the legal framework for AI‑trained content remains unsettled, exposing creators to infringement risks and unintended deep‑fake applications.

## How to think about it
Treat AI licensing as a strategic partnership: start by defining clear usage boundaries, retain attribution rights, and negotiate revenue shares that reflect the long‑term value of the data. Conduct a risk assessment to identify scenarios where your footage could be misrepresented, and include audit clauses that let you monitor how the material is employed. Finally, stay informed about evolving legal precedents and consider consulting intellectual‑property counsel before signing aggregator agreements.

<Callout type="warning">Do not sign a blanket license that grants unlimited, perpetual use of your footage without safeguards for attribution, context, and revenue sharing.</Callout>
