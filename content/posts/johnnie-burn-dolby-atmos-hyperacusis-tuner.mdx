Johnnie Burn, the Oscar‑winning sound designer behind Hamnet and The Zone of Interest, faced a unique challenge when Daniel Roher handed him the script for Tuner. The film follows Niki, a piano tuner whose hyperacusis makes everyday sounds excruciatingly loud. Burn turned his own past experience with mild hyperacusis into a research mission, recording sounds from inside his own ears to understand how ordinary noises can feel overwhelming. By layering those recordings in a Dolby Atmos mix, he let audiences hear the world as Niki does, turning a medical condition into a narrative device. The result is a visceral soundscape that deepens empathy and pushes immersive cinema forward.

<Callout type="takeaway">Dolby Atmos lets Burn turn hyperacusis into an audible experience, making the condition a central storytelling element.</Callout>

## What happened
Burn began by placing miniature microphones inside his eardrums, capturing daily sounds such as a dishwasher clank or a shower tap from the perspective of someone with heightened sensitivity. He then built a multi‑layered Atmos mix that placed these sounds at varying distances and intensities, mimicking how Niki’s brain amplifies otherwise mundane noises. The final mix includes a “hyper‑aware” track that follows Niki’s point of view and a conventional track for the rest of the audience, allowing both to coexist in the same theater.

The approach required close collaboration with the director and the mix team to ensure the heightened audio cues aligned with the visual narrative. Burn’s work not only serves the plot—showing why Niki is drawn to safe‑cracking—but also educates viewers about the daily reality of hyperacusis.

## Why it matters
By translating a medical condition into an immersive audio experience, the film raises public awareness of hyperacusis, a condition many have never heard of. The technique demonstrates how sound design can function as a narrative character, shaping audience perception beyond dialogue and visuals. It also sets a benchmark for future productions that aim to represent sensory disorders authentically, encouraging studios to invest in advanced spatial audio tools.

## How to think about it
Filmmakers looking to embed sensory experiences should start with personal research or first‑hand accounts of the condition. Capture audio in the environment of the subject, even using unconventional methods like in‑ear microphones. Map those recordings onto a spatial audio platform, assigning distance and volume cues that reflect the character’s perception. Test the mix with a small audience to gauge comfort levels, then refine the balance between immersion and accessibility.

<Callout type="warning">Audiences with hyperacusis or sound‑sensitivity disorders may find the Atmos mix overwhelming; provide a standard stereo version or a volume warning when possible.</Callout>
