During a recent episode of the “Inside of You” podcast, Dafne Keen opened up about being offered cocaine at wrap parties when she was just 11‑13 years old. The revelation comes after her breakout performance as Laura in Logan, a role that catapulted her into the Hollywood spotlight. Keen’s account shines a light on the often‑overlooked adult environments that surround young talent. It also raises questions about how studios protect minors on set. Understanding her experience helps fans and industry insiders gauge the hidden pressures behind a child star’s success.

<Callout type="takeaway">Even young actors can encounter drug offers on set, but personal boundaries can keep them safe.</Callout>

## What happened

Keen told host Michael Rosenbaum, “I was getting offered coke at wrap parties when I was tiny… like 11, 12, 13,” during the podcast. She added, “I don’t really have any interest in that,” emphasizing her refusal to partake. The comments line up with the timeline of Logan’s release, when Keen was 12, and illustrate the unexpected adult‑only gatherings that can surround a child star’s schedule.

She later noted that the experience left her with a “no‑interest” stance toward hard drinking, describing herself as “such a control freak” when it comes to alcohol. Rosenbaum reacted with surprise, admitting he didn’t fully understand what cocaine was until after college, underscoring how out‑of‑place the offers felt for both parties.

## Why it matters

Keen’s disclosure spotlights a broader industry issue: the exposure of minors to adult‑centric party culture, where substances like cocaine may be present. When a child actor is thrust into high‑profile projects, the line between professional responsibilities and social settings can blur, increasing the risk of inappropriate offers. This raises ethical questions for studios, agents, and guardians about the duty of care owed to young performers and the need for stricter oversight at wrap celebrations.

## How to think about it

Parents and guardians should establish firm rules about after‑hours events and require chaperones for any post‑shoot gatherings. Production companies can create a “substance‑free” policy for wrap parties, clearly communicating expectations to all staff. Actors themselves can benefit from mentorship programs that teach how to decline offers confidently, as Keen demonstrated. By combining clear policies, adult supervision, and open dialogue, the industry can reduce the likelihood of minors encountering illicit substances.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume every wrap party involves drugs; each production’s culture varies, and blanket accusations can unfairly damage reputations.</Callout>
