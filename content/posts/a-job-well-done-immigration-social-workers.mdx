A Job Well Done, Fred Baillif’s latest hybrid feature, drops into the heart of Geneva’s asylum reception centre and follows social workers as they juggle life‑saving empathy with a maze of red tape. The film blends documentary footage with scripted scenes, letting real‑world cases unfold on a double‑decker bus that serves as a mobile intake point. Its world premiere at the Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival puts the Swiss migration debate on an international stage. By foregrounding the moral friction between compassion and legal constraints, the movie asks viewers to reconsider the human cost of bureaucratic inertia. The story matters because it mirrors broader global tensions over refugee assistance.

<Callout type="takeaway">Compassion and bureaucracy collide, revealing the hidden human toll of immigration policies.</Callout>

## What happened
The film premiered on November 14 in the Official Selection Competition at the 30th Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival, drawing attention to Switzerland’s increasingly complex asylum system. Baillif, a former basketball player turned social‑work educator, built the narrative from immersive research at the Association of Intercultural Mediators and the Roseraie migration centre, integrating interviews and improvisation workshops with actual case files.

In the story, a double‑decker bus becomes a mobile front line where social workers meet migrants seeking shelter, legal advice, or simply a listening ear. The crew captures moments of negotiation, desperation, and occasional triumph as staff balance institutional rules with the urgent need to protect vulnerable lives.

## Why it matters
The clash between humanitarian impulse and rigid bureaucracy highlighted in the film reflects a universal dilemma faced by many host countries. When policies prioritize procedure over people, frontline workers become moral arbiters, and the consequences ripple through refugee communities, public opinion, and political discourse. Understanding this tension helps viewers grasp why immigration reform often stalls and why compassionate action can be perceived as rule‑breaking.

## How to think about it
When watching the film, consider each scene as a case study of systemic pressure rather than a definitive solution. Ask yourself how the depicted bureaucratic hurdles compare to those in your own country, and what small policy adjustments could ease the burden on both migrants and social workers. Use the narrative as a springboard for broader conversations about humane immigration frameworks.

<Callout type="warning">Do not treat the film as a policy handbook; it dramatizes personal stories rather than offering systematic solutions.</Callout>
