Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim’s hybrid documentary “You Can See Everything” arrived on streaming platforms this week, inviting audiences into the tangled lives of Elizabeth Holmes and her partner Billy Evans. Filmed almost entirely inside a cramped San Diego beach house, the film turns the domestic setting into a claustrophobic stage for psychological drama. Critics quickly noted that beyond its investigative angle, the work functions as a modern gothic romance, echoing classics like Rebecca and Mulholland Drive. By positioning Holmes as a vulnerable heroine and Evans as a dangerous lover, the documentary revives the genre’s themes of deception, obsession, and haunted intimacy. This framing reshapes how documentary filmmakers can blend narrative tropes with real‑world scrutiny.

<Callout type="takeaway">The film proves that documentary can be a gothic romance, using real lives to dramatize timeless themes of love and betrayal.</Callout>

## What happened
You Can See Everything combines documentary footage with staged interviews to explore truth, performance, and authenticity. The project follows Holmes weeks before her imprisonment, documenting her interactions with her children and with Billy Evans, a gun‑wielding partner who mirrors her volatility.

The San Diego beach house serves as a psychological chamber; tight framing and constant camera presence turn everyday moments into unsettling set pieces. As the narrative unfolds, the relationship between Holmes and Evans evolves into a folie à deux, leaving viewers unsure who manipulates whom.

## Why it matters
By casting a real‑world scandal in the language of gothic romance, the film expands the documentary form beyond pure reportage, inviting viewers to feel the emotional stakes of deception and love. This hybrid approach challenges traditional genre boundaries, influencing future creators who may blend factual investigation with narrative archetypes. Audiences gain a deeper, more visceral understanding of Holmes’s public persona and the dynamics of power that underlie high‑profile fraud cases.

## How to think about it
Approach the film as a layered narrative: first, assess the factual timeline of Holmes’s legal situation; second, observe how visual composition and editing create gothic motifs; third, reflect on how the emotional undercurrents influence your perception of truth. This three‑step framework helps separate documentary evidence from artistic embellishment, allowing a balanced appreciation of both form and content.
